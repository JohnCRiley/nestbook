Scotland's 2026 Season: A Highland Tourism Story of Resilience, Growth, and a New Question About Who Pays

NB
The NestBook Team
September 2026
· 6 min read
This blog is written in English. For other languages, your browser's built-in translation tool works well — right-click anywhere on the page and select "Translate".

There's a particular kind of beauty in the Scottish Highlands that doesn't photograph the same way twice. The light on Loch Ness changes by the hour. The Cairngorms hold snow long after the rest of Britain has forgotten what it looks like. The North Coast 500 unspools past sea lochs and single-track roads that feel, for a moment, like the edge of the world. It's little wonder millions of people plan their year around getting there.

2026 has been a complicated, human year for that part of the world — one with real growth, a genuine crisis, and an honest conversation now underway about how a place protects what makes it worth visiting in the first place. If you run a B&B, a self-catering cottage, or a small guesthouse anywhere near the Highlands, all three threads are worth understanding.

A season of real international growth

By most measures, Scotland is having a strong year. International flight bookings to Scotland were up 15% year-on-year, even as bookings to London and other parts of England fell over the same period. International visitor spending told the same story — Visa card spending by overseas visitors rose 19% year-on-year in the first quarter of 2026, with the United States now Scotland's largest international market by spending.

Tourism remains one of the country's most important industries. It's estimated to contribute around 5% of Scotland's GDP and support roughly 218,000 jobs — about 8% of total employment. In the Highlands and Islands specifically, that share runs much higher: in some communities along the NC500, in Skye, and in similarly tourism-dependent areas, tourism accounts for more than 20% of the local economy. When a place's identity and its income are this tightly bound together, every good season and every hard one is felt personally, not just economically.

Domestically, the picture has been a little more mixed. Survey data from early in the year showed holiday intentions among UK residents softening as 2026 went on — from around 80% in January down to 76% by late April — with rising living costs, fuel prices, and general inflation cited as the main dampeners. Domestic holidaymaking has stayed comparatively resilient even so, more so than outbound European travel, which has taken more of the hit. It's a familiar pattern for anyone watching UK travel this year: international visitors arriving in strong numbers and spending freely, domestic guests being a little more careful with where and how they spend.

The Cairngorms wildfire: a hard few weeks for Badenoch and Strathspey

Any honest account of the Highlands' 2026 season has to include what happened in July.

On 15 July, a wildfire broke out in the Cairngorms National Park, spreading through Glenmore and the area around An Lochan Uaine. It burned for over two weeks, was described by those who witnessed it as apocalyptic in scale, and caused lasting damage to ancient Caledonian pine forest — a landscape that takes centuries to recover, not seasons. Homes were evacuated. Roads and trails were closed at the very height of the summer season.

The human and business toll was significant and immediate. Seventeen businesses in Glenmore remained closed in the fire's aftermath, with community leaders warning that more than 200 local jobs were at risk. Glenmore Campsite alone issued over 550 refunds to guests who could no longer visit. Industry bodies were candid that the economic impact, while impossible to fully quantify in the moment, would be real and lasting for accommodation providers, cafes, activity operators, and their suppliers across the area.

The response was substantial too. The Scottish Government confirmed up to £3 million in funding, administered through Highland Council, to help affected businesses stay afloat and rebuild. Drop-in support sessions were run in Aviemore and Nethy Bridge, bringing together the council, NHS Highland, the fire service, and local support organisations for residents and business owners trying to figure out what came next.

It's worth saying plainly, because tourism boards were keen to say it too: the vast majority of businesses across the Cairngorms National Park remained open and ready to welcome visitors throughout. A crisis in one valley is not a crisis across an entire national park — but for the people directly affected, that distinction offers little comfort, and it deserves acknowledging rather than smoothing over in a tourism roundup.

A new conversation: the Highland Visitor Levy

Against that backdrop, Highland Council has spent much of 2026 working through a genuinely significant policy question: should the Highlands introduce a visitor levy, and if so, what should it look like?

This isn't a new idea — the council has reportedly been exploring some version of a tourist tax for around seven years. What changed in 2026 is the legal framework. Amendments to the Visitor Levy (Scotland) Act came into force on 22 July, giving local authorities more flexibility in how they structure a levy — specifically, the choice between a percentage-based charge (the model Edinburgh introduced) or a fixed amount per room, per night.

Highland Council has opted for the fixed-rate model. Under the draft proposal that went to a special council meeting on 25 August 2026:

£5 per room or accommodation area, per night — applies to hotels, guest houses, B&Bs, rooms in private homes, self-catering properties, and similar short-term let accommodation

£2 per room, per night — applies to hostels, campsites, and caravan parks

Nine months of the year — the levy would run year-round excluding December, January, and February

5% retained by the accommodation provider — to help cover administration costs

£9–9.5 million estimated annual revenue — earmarked for tourism infrastructure, including roads and facilities relied on by nearly 9.4 million annual visitors to the Highlands

To be clear about where things actually stand: no decision has been made to introduce this levy. Following the August meeting, Highland Council opened a full 12-week statutory public consultation, inviting residents, businesses, and visitors to share their views. That consultation feeds into a report due back to full council on 10 December 2026, when councillors will decide whether to proceed at all. Even if approved, a statutory 18-month implementation period would follow — meaning, realistically, nothing would actually take effect before 2028.

The Association of Scotland's Self-Caterers (ASSC) has been actively engaging with the proposals and encouraging its members to take part in the consultation directly, which is worth doing if you run a self-catering property anywhere in the Highland Council area — this is exactly the stage in the process where an operator's actual experience carries the most weight.

What this means if you run a small property here

If you're watching this process from inside a B&B, a gîte-style cottage, or a small guesthouse in the Highlands, a few things are worth holding onto:

Nothing changes tomorrow. Between the consultation, the December council decision, and the 18-month implementation window that would follow approval, there's a long runway even in the fastest-moving scenario. There's time to understand it properly before it affects a single booking.

The structure matters for planning. A flat £5-per-room-per-night charge behaves very differently from a percentage-based one, especially across a season with real rate variation between a quiet April midweek and a peak-August weekend. If you do end up needing to add a levy line to your pricing one day, a fixed nightly amount is at least a simpler thing to build into a rate calendar than a moving percentage would be.

Every pound collected outside a commission platform is a pound you keep the full record of. Whatever happens with the levy, it will apply per room, per night — which means accurate, direct visibility into your own bookings and occupancy isn't just good practice, it becomes the thing that makes correctly collecting and reporting any future levy straightforward rather than a scramble.

The Highlands' resilience this year has been real, not just resilient marketing copy. A landscape and a community that could absorb a genuine crisis in July and still be having a strong international season by late summer says something honest about the place and the people who run its small businesses. That's not a small thing to be part of.

Scotland's Highlands remain one of the most quietly remarkable places in the world to build a small hospitality business — and 2026 has been a year that tested that community and, by most measures, found it standing. Whatever comes of the visitor levy consultation, the businesses that come out of it in the best shape will likely be the ones who already have a clear, direct line to their own guests, their own bookings, and their own numbers — rather than depending on someone else's platform to tell them how their season actually went.
