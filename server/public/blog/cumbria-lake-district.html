Fells, Fire and Fast Boats: The Story of Cumbria and the Lake District

NB
The NestBook Team
August 2026
· 6 min read

There's a reason people fall for Cumbria and don't fall out of love with it. It's not just the scenery, though the scenery does most of the early work — glacial lakes laid out between fells that change colour by the hour, stone villages that look like they grew out of the hillside rather than being built on it, and a quiet that's genuinely hard to find anywhere else in England. It's also a place with a properly good story behind it: mountaineers and monument-makers, world speed records won and lost on the same lake, and a village just over the border that became famous for entirely different reasons.

This is the region at its best — what it offers today, where it came from, and what 2026 has looked like on the ground.

What the Lake District actually offers

At its core, the Lake District is England's largest national park and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, built around sixteen major lakes and hundreds of named fells. Windermere, England's longest lake at over 17 kilometres, anchors the southern end of the park and remains the single most-visited stretch of water in the region — its lake cruises are consistently one of the most popular paid attractions anywhere in the North West of England. Further north, Keswick sits beneath Skiddaw and on the shore of Derwentwater, acting as the natural base for walkers heading into the northern fells. Ambleside and Grasmere, both tucked into the central valleys, carry the literary weight of the region — Wordsworth country, in the truest sense, with Dove Cottage and Rydal Mount still drawing visitors who want to stand where the poetry was actually written.

But the real substance of the Lake District has always been underfoot rather than in the towns: 214 named fells, hundreds of miles of footpath, and a landscape genuinely built for walking, climbing, sailing and, increasingly, wild swimming. It's a working landscape too — sheep farming and dry-stone walling shaped these fells long before tourism did, and that agricultural backbone is still very visible if you look for it.

A few pieces of history worth knowing

Alfred Wainwright is the reason so many of the fells even have a shared identity today. Between 1955 and 1966, Wainwright hand-wrote and hand-illustrated his seven-volume Pictorial Guide to the Lakeland Fells, covering 214 named peaks that are now simply known as "the Wainwrights" in his honour. Over two million copies of his guides have been sold since, and completing all 214 remains a genuine badge of honour among Lake District walkers to this day.

Coniston Water has its own, considerably more adrenaline-fuelled history. Sir Malcolm Campbell set a world water speed record here in 1939, reaching 141mph in his boat Bluebird. His son, Donald Campbell, went on to break the water speed record on Coniston four separate times through the late 1950s, eventually pushing past 260mph — before returning to the lake in January 1967 in an attempt to become the first person to break 300mph on water. He didn't survive the attempt. His body and the wreckage of Bluebird K7 weren't recovered from the lake until 2001, and Coniston's small parish churchyard remains his final resting place.

Scafell Pike, rising to 978 metres in the Southern Fells, is England's highest mountain — and sits, fittingly, at the head of Wasdale, one of the wildest and least-visited valleys in the whole national park.

Gretna Green isn't technically in Cumbria at all — it sits just over the Scottish border, the very first village travellers reach heading north — but it's so tightly bound up with journeys through this part of England that it's impossible to leave out. After England's Marriage Act of 1754 required anyone under 21 to have parental consent to marry, Scottish law required nothing of the sort, and Gretna Green's blacksmith's shop became the destination of choice for eloping couples racing the border. The tradition of the blacksmith striking his anvil to seal a marriage is where the phrase "anvil priest" comes from, and the Old Smithy — now a museum as well as a working wedding venue — still marries couples over that same anvil more than 270 years later.

The tourist trail — and where to go instead

Windermere, Bowness and Ambleside are popular for good reason, and nobody should feel talked out of visiting them. But they're also where the crowds concentrate hardest, especially in peak summer weeks — and a short drive in almost any direction opens up a genuinely different pace.

Buttermere and its quieter neighbour Loweswater, tucked into the northwest of the park, offer the same dramatic lake-and-fell scenery with a fraction of the footfall — and Buttermere's natural "infinity pool," a short walk from the main car park, has become a genuine wild-swimming favourite for those in the know. Rannerdale Knotts, nearby, is worth timing a visit around late April, when its slopes turn a startling blue with wild bluebells. Ennerdale Water, further west still, is remote enough that visitors often have long stretches of shoreline entirely to themselves, with views that stretch out toward the Irish Sea on a clear day.

For something quieter closer to the honeypots, Blea Tarn and the nearby Cathedral Quarry — a disused slate cavern in the Langdale valley, with light pouring in through a hole in its roof — sit a short, little-known detour from the busy Ambleside–Langdale road. And Gummer's How, at the southern tip of Windermere itself, offers one of the best viewpoints over the lake for a fraction of the walking effort required elsewhere in the park.

What 2026 looked like on the ground

This year has, by most accounts, been one of the busiest in Cumbria's recent history. Local tourism bodies and transport operators described a genuine "tourism boom" across the county this summer, with one summer weekend flagged as likely the busiest the national park had seen in at least four years — accommodation providers in Windermere, Ambleside, Bowness, Keswick and Grasmere reported running close to full capacity, and the roads feeding into the region (the M6, A590, A66 and A591) carried some of the heaviest holiday traffic seen since before the pandemic.

Rail operators felt it too. Northern, which runs the Lakes Line into Windermere, expected more than half a million journeys across 2026/27 — a genuine record — as growing numbers of visitors chose the train over the car amid the same uncertainty around international travel driving staycations across the rest of the UK. A new Cumbria Travel Pass, allowing visitors to combine trains, buses and boats on a single ticket, launched this year specifically in response to that demand, aimed at easing congestion by encouraging people to leave the car behind.

Behind the headline numbers, the underlying trend has been building for a few years already. In the most recent full-year figures available, the Lake District recorded 18.11 million visitors and 27.21 million "tourist days," generating £2.3 billion for the local economy — and the visitor profile has been quietly getting younger and more diverse, with the proportion of 16–34-year-old visitors climbing and visits from minority ethnic communities rising sharply since the pandemic. This isn't a region riding on nostalgia. It's a working tourist economy that's actively growing its audience, not just recycling the same one.

What this means if you run an independent property in Cumbria

If you're a B&B, guesthouse or cottage owner anywhere in the Lakes, the story behind this year's numbers is genuinely encouraging: demand is real, it's broadening beyond the traditional visitor profile, and the crowding around the honeypot towns is, if anything, pushing more visitors to look further afield for where they stay — which favours smaller, independent properties outside the busiest few postcodes just as much as those inside them.
