Britain's Holiday Boom, Take Two: What's Really Driving the 2026 Staycation Surge

NB
The NestBook Team
August 2026
· 7 min read

There's a strange sense of déjà vu running through Britain's holiday season this year. Coastal cottages are booked out months in advance. Lake District farmhouses are turning guests away. Cornwall's harbour towns are heaving in a way that feels, on the surface, exactly like the Britain your grandparents would recognise — caravans queuing for the coast, families choosing Devon over the Dordogne, "staying local" as the default rather than the exception.

But scratch the surface, and this isn't a simple return to the past. It's a genuinely different phenomenon wearing a familiar coat — driven by cost, currency swings and a changed set of choices that has almost nothing in common with why your grandparents stayed home.

The first golden age: staying home because there was nowhere else to go

To understand why 2026 feels significant, it helps to remember what British holidaymaking actually looked like the last time domestic travel dominated so completely.

The British seaside holiday came into its true heyday in the 1950s and 60s, made possible for ordinary working families by the Holiday Pay Act of 1938, which for the first time guaranteed paid annual leave to millions of workers. Where you went depended on where you lived — mill towns emptied into Blackpool and Morecambe, Leeds families headed for Scarborough, Londoners took the train to Brighton or Margate. Foreign travel wasn't a rejected option; for most families it barely existed as an option at all. No passport, no unfamiliar currency, no unfamiliar food — just a train, a coach, or eventually the family car, and a boarding house by the sea.

That era peaked in the early-to-mid 1970s, when just over 40 million domestic holidays were taken in a single year — genuinely the high-water mark of British seaside tourism. And then it fell apart, fast. The arrival of the jet age and cheap package holidays to Spain in the 1970s and 80s offered guaranteed sunshine at a price ordinary families could suddenly afford. By 1987 — the worst year on record for the British seaside — resort boarding houses were switching from holidaymakers to housing the unemployed, as the "Costa del Dole" years hollowed out towns that had spent a century building an identity around the British holiday.

The story that gets told about that decline is simple: Brits found somewhere better and left. The real story is a little more layered — burgeoning car ownership was already pulling visitors toward rural destinations even before package holidays existed, and much of the "golden age" nostalgia is itself a rose-tinted retelling of a period that was, at the time, considered fairly ordinary. But the broad arc holds: for thirty years, going abroad became easier, cheaper and more aspirational than staying home, and the British seaside spent the following decades trying to reinvent itself in response.

What changed in between: a weaker pound and a different kind of border

Skip forward to 2016 and the decision to leave the EU, and the picture starts to shift again — though not in a straight line.

In the immediate aftermath of that vote, the pound fell sharply against the dollar and euro, and — briefly — this was genuinely good news for British tourism. Inbound flight bookings to the UK rose within weeks, as international visitors realised a British holiday had just got noticeably cheaper. VisitBritain's own leadership described the effect at the time as a real, if temporary, tourism boost.

Since then, the picture has been more mixed. A new Electronic Travel Authorisation now applies to most European visitors, with the fee rising to £16, replacing the identity-card route over 200 million EU citizens had previously used to enter the UK. Extra steps and extra cost at the border tend to blunt spontaneous, short-notice European trips — the kind that used to make up a meaningful share of inbound visits — and VisitBritain's own forecasting has flagged this as a genuine headwind to the UK holding its competitive share of European travel. On the flip side, the same weaker pound that helps inbound visitors works in reverse for British holidaymakers heading the other way — every euro or dollar simply buys a little less than it used to, which nudges the maths gently back toward staying home.

The 2026 surge: not nostalgia, a rational response to the moment

Which brings us to this summer — and the numbers turned out to be genuinely striking.

Roughly 80% of British travellers chose a UK staycation this year over a holiday abroad. More than 25 million Brits skipped foreign travel entirely in favour of staying in the UK, with the Lake District, Peak District, Devon and Cornwall topping the list of most sought-after destinations. Industry researchers pointed to two clear drivers: rising international flight costs, and the "revenge travel" boom that fuelled a surge in overseas bookings for the three years after the pandemic finally running its course.

The scale of that shift shows up in real bookings, not just survey answers. Deborah Lyon, who runs The Heaning Estate in the Lake District — eleven independent holiday properties — reported bookings up 30% year-to-date, with guests already booking ahead into 2027. The UK staycation market as a whole was valued at £25.3 million in 2025 and is projected to grow at over 6% a year. And it wasn't a one-off summer pilgrimage the way it often was in the 1960s: UK adults now take an average of three domestic breaks a year, and short breaks of six nights or fewer made up 48% of all UK stays this year, up from 41% just three years ago. 2026 wasn't one big annual holiday at home — it was several smaller ones, taken more often, and booked later. The average lead time for a UK cottage-style stay fell to around 105 days.

The other half of the story: Britain wants to be a powerhouse again

Here's the part that makes 2026 genuinely different from a simple retreat to safety: alongside millions of individual households quietly choosing to stay closer to home, there's a very deliberate, government-backed push happening in the other direction — to make Britain a global tourism powerhouse again, not just a fallback for its own citizens.

VisitBritain and the UK government have set a public target of 50 million international visitors a year by 2030, up from a forecast 43–44 million in 2025. The agency's "Starring GREAT Britain" campaign — leaning on the international pull of British film and TV locations — is credited with generating an estimated £217 million in additional international visitor spending in its first six months alone, and VisitBritain's broader activity is credited with adding £562 million to the economy in the 2024–25 financial year, 60% of it outside London. The pitch isn't subtle: tourism is being positioned as one of the clearest, fastest levers available to demonstrate that Britain is still a serious, outward-facing destination — soft power and economic growth rolled into one campaign.

So Britain in 2026 has, in a sense, been pulling in two directions at once. British holidaymakers turned inward this year, prioritising value, certainty and familiarity closer to home. And the national tourism strategy has simultaneously been trying to project outward confidence to the rest of the world. Both are responses to the same underlying moment — cost pressure, currency swings, a world that feels a little less predictable than it did a few years ago — just aimed at different audiences.

The real difference between then and now

It's tempting to describe this as history repeating itself. It isn't, not really. The 1960s British holidaymaker stayed home because going abroad was expensive, unfamiliar and genuinely difficult to arrange. The 2026 British holidaymaker chose to stay home despite it being easier than ever to go abroad — cheap flights exist, budget airlines exist, package holidays exist — because the maths simply worked out differently this year. And, cottage by cottage, farmhouse by farmhouse, the quality and range of what's on offer at home has genuinely improved — this isn't 1987's Costa del Dole guesthouses standing in as a fallback. It's architect-renovated barns, adventure-driven Lake District estates, and independently run properties competing seriously on their own merits, not just on the absence of an alternative.

That's the real story of 2026: not Britain rediscovering an old habit, but British holidaymakers making a genuinely modern, deliberate choice — for reasons their grandparents would recognise (value, familiarity, certainty) but arriving there by an entirely different route.

What this means if you run an independent UK property

If you own a B&B, cottage, or small guesthouse anywhere in the UK, the underlying demand story here is good news, and it's not a blip. Guests are booking more often, in shorter bursts, closer to home, and later — which rewards properties with an easy, fast, direct way to book rather than a lengthy back-and-forth. With booking windows shrinking and repeat visits rising, the properties that win are the ones that make it effortless for a guest to check availability and book on the spot, without losing a chunk of the booking to commission along the way.
